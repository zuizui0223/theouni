# Audit — CREST and restoration-monitoring budget heuristics

## Question

Does CREST prohibit an inference that is actually close to restoration practice, or only a hypothetical inference no practitioner makes?

## Evidence located

### U.S. EPA wetland-restoration valuation case study

A 2002 U.S. EPA case-study cost table calculated monitoring as five percent of the total of land acquisition, land transaction, restoration action, and maintenance. This is direct evidence that proportional costing can be used as an administrative estimation rule in a restoration context.

### National Academies restoration-monitoring review

The 2017 National Academies report *Effective Monitoring to Evaluate Ecological Restoration in the Gulf of Mexico* summarizes an earlier project review in which monitoring averaged thirteen percent of total project cost, with a range from three to sixty-seven percent. The report also stresses that monitoring design and statistical confidence are related and that critical activities beyond field collection can be underfunded.

## Safe interpretation

The sources support three claims:

1. restoration-monitoring budgets may be expressed as a fraction of broader project cost;
2. realized monitoring fractions vary widely across projects;
3. project cost alone does not encode the response distinctions a monitoring design must resolve.

The CREST theorem supplies a formal reason for claim 3. A fixed small capability or carrier gain can coexist with arbitrary required-state and monitoring-resolution growth.

## Prohibited interpretation

Do not claim:

- that EPA, the National Academies, or restoration practitioners asserted the mathematical implication `small intervention => small epistemic burden`;
- that five percent, thirteen percent, or any other fraction is intrinsically wrong;
- that CREST determines an optimal monitoring budget;
- that the historical three-to-sixty-seven-percent range proves the CREST theorem empirically;
- that monitoring expenditure and CREST monitoring debt are the same quantity.

## Chapter wording locked by this audit

Use:

> Scale-based budgeting can be administratively useful, but project cost, area, or intervention count cannot by itself certify that monitoring resolves all response distinctions made relevant by the management repertoire.

Avoid:

> Current restoration budgeting is mathematically invalid.

## Scientific payoff

This connection removes the strongest straw-man risk without overselling practical impact. The theorem does not replace budget planning. It prevents a budget variable from being mistaken for an epistemic guarantee and motivates response-based re-auditing when management capability changes.
