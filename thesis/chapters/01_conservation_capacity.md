# Chapter 1 — When Conservation Capacity Outgrows Conservation Knowledge

**Status:** source-bounded writing brief  
**Primary owner:** `zuizui0223/crest`

## Problem

Conservation planning often treats project scale, restored area, or intervention count as a practical basis for monitoring allocation. This chapter asks whether a small increase in what management can do also bounds the additional state resolution needed to use that capability responsibly.

## Headline result

For every integer \(m\ge1\), CREST constructs a finite family in which one newly admitted controllable action adds exactly one viable world while forcing a retained present slice to refine from one state to \(2^m\) states. Under unchanged evidence, monitoring debt is exactly \(m\) bits.

## Why the result is nontrivial

The important claim is not that state depends on purpose or action. It is the no-bound separation between a fixed small capability gain and an arbitrarily large representational burden.

## Ecological payoff

Intervention scale may remain a useful administrative budgeting variable, but it cannot certify that the monitoring design resolves all distinctions made relevant by the new management repertoire.

## Claim ceiling

Do not claim that real conservation programmes generally exhibit exponential monitoring growth, that budget heuristics are administratively irrational, or that the finite witness empirically validates a restoration system. The theorem is an impossibility boundary absent additional ecological structure.
